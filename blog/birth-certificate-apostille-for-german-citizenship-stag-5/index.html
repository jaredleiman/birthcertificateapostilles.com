Birth Certificate Apostilles for German Citizenship by Descent (StAG §5)

Germany's 2021 StAG §5 reform opened citizenship to descendants previously excluded. The application window runs through 2031. Apostilled birth certificates are part of the document chain.

Request a Quote

Germany's August 2021 reform of the Staatsangehörigkeitsgesetz, known as StAG §5, opened citizenship by descent to categories of applicants who had previously been excluded under earlier interpretations of German nationality law. The reform corrected long-standing inequalities tied to gender, marital status at the time of a parent's birth, and Nazi-era denaturalizations that had cascaded across generations. The application window runs through August 2031, after which the special pathway closes and applicants revert to the standard descent rules.

The dossier required for a StAG §5 application is document-heavy, and apostilled US birth certificates are part of nearly every case where the applicant or an intermediate ancestor was born in the United States. This guide covers what StAG §5 actually covers, how birth certificate apostilles fit into the chain, and the patterns that delay applications.

Who StAG §5 Covers

The reform created a declarative path to citizenship, Erklärungserwerb, for several previously excluded groups. The most common categories among US-based applicants:

Descendants of German mothers and non-German fathers, born before 1975. Prior to a 1975 reform, children took citizenship only from the father. A child born in 1965 to a German-born mother and a US-born father did not acquire German citizenship at birth under the rules then in effect. StAG §5 corrects this for those descendants and their further descendants.

Descendants of German fathers and non-German mothers, born outside marriage, before 1993. Children born out of wedlock to German fathers were excluded from automatic citizenship under earlier rules. StAG §5 opens citizenship to these descendants.

Descendants of Germans who lost citizenship through marriage to a non-German. German women who married non-German men before April 1953 lost their German citizenship by operation of law. Their descendants were therefore born to a non-German mother, even though the mother had been German by birth. StAG §5 addresses these cascading exclusions.

Descendants of Germans denaturalized between 1933 and 1945. A separate pathway under StAG §15 already exists for descendants of Nazi-era denaturalization victims. StAG §5 covers related fact patterns where the §15 standard does not apply.

Each category has its own evidentiary requirements, but birth certificates apostilled for foreign use are part of nearly every case where the applicant or an intermediate generation was born in the United States.

The Document Chain

A StAG §5 application typically requires birth certificates for the applicant, for each US-born intermediate ancestor in the chain, and for the German-born ancestor whose citizenship is being claimed. The German-born ancestor's birth record comes from German archives separately and does not need US authentication.

For US-born documents in the chain, the requirement is a long-form certified copy from the issuing state, apostilled by that state's Secretary of State. Short-form certificates are not accepted. The long-form is required because the parental information it contains is what connects each generation in the chain to the next.

Marriage certificates often enter the chain alongside birth certificates. The 1953 marriage that caused a German mother to lose citizenship, for example, is a fact that has to be documented if the StAG §5 claim is based on that loss. US marriage certificates from any state in the chain need their own apostilles, following the same state-level routing.

Divorce decrees, death certificates, and name-change records can also enter the chain depending on the family history. Each follows the same state-level apostille rule as birth and marriage certificates.

Sworn Translation: Beglaubigte Übersetzung

German authorities require certified translation, beglaubigte Übersetzung, by a translator authorized by a German court, öffentlich bestellter und beeidigter Übersetzer. US-based certified translations are not accepted at most German authorities reviewing StAG §5 applications.

The certified translator can be located through the German court interpreter database, Dolmetscher- und Übersetzerdatenbank, maintained jointly by the German federal and state justice administrations. Many sworn translators work remotely and accept scanned documents. Translation timelines vary by language pair and document length but typically run one to three weeks.

Some German consulates in the United States maintain their own lists of approved translators and may accept translations from translators on those lists in lieu of court-authorized translators in Germany. Practice varies. The Bundesverwaltungsamt, which processes most StAG §5 applications, generally prefers translations from court-authorized translators based in Germany.

Freshness Considerations

StAG §5 applications do not enforce a strict freshness window on birth certificates the way Spanish or Italian authorities do. A birth certificate apostilled some years ago can often be reused, provided it was a long-form certified copy at the time of issuance.

The exception is where the application is filed alongside or in coordination with a residency matter inside Germany, in which case the supporting documents may carry their own freshness rules. For applications filed at German consulates abroad with no in-country residency component, freshness is generally not the binding constraint.

Where Applications Stall

StAG §5 applications stalled on apostille issues cluster around a few patterns.

Short-form documents in the chain. Applicant pulled short-form birth certificates for one or more generations in the chain. The Bundesverwaltungsamt requests long-form versions. Each replacement adds weeks to the timeline, particularly for older records or records from states with backlogged vital records offices.

Marriage chain incomplete. Applicant assembled birth certificates for every generation but overlooked a marriage that explains a surname change in the chain. German authorities require the surname continuity to be documented.

Translation by wrong type of translator. US-based certified translations submitted to the Bundesverwaltungsamt or to a German consulate, returned with a request for translation by a court-authorized translator. The translation has to be redone, sometimes from scratch.

Older state records harder to pull. Birth certificates from the 1920s, 1930s, and 1940s, which are common in StAG §5 cases involving older intermediate generations, are sometimes archived rather than held in routine vital records databases. Pulling these requires special requests and can take significantly longer than current records.
